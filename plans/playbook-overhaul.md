Status: draft for review. Generated by Claude (Fable 5.1) on 2026-10-01 from a full read of the Playbook, the Technology SharePoint "Ways of Working" and "Service Desk" folders, and the Knowledgebase.

## 1. What the Playbook is for

The Playbook is the public statement of how AM's Technology department works: what we value, how we turn ideas into released software, and the standards we hold ourselves to. Its readers are people in the department, people elsewhere in AM who work with us, candidates, and anyone curious. It is deliberately public so that we can be held to it.

It is not the place for runbooks, tool configuration, repository names, internal links, templates, or anything that only makes sense inside the department. That belongs in the Knowledgebase (internal) or SharePoint.

## 3. Current practice the Playbook must describe

Drawn from the SharePoint documents and the Knowledgebase. The Playbook states these at the level of principle and shape; the detail stays where it is.

**Roadmap and objectives.** Anyone can write a proposal. Proposals are validated for viability (is it worth doing now) and feasibility (can we do it), then become Candidates. Candidates are prioritised into Now, Next and Later; the roadmap shows about nine months. An objective is scoped to roughly three months or less and has a canonical objective document: summary, problem statement, scope, out of scope, key results, stakeholders, outcomes. Scope is cut hard; extensions become stretch outcomes or future objectives. A team usually has one objective in active implementation at a time.

**Lifecycle.** Four phases: discovery, design, implementation, operation. Discovery is open, aligned to strategy, evidence-led, rapid and disciplined. Design is prescriptive about outcomes and flexible in process: high-level design (scope, solution design, outcome specifications, value delivery framework) completes before implementation; detailed design (backlog items, UX, technical specifications, incremental delivery plan) continues alongside it. Outcome specifications are written from the user's perspective, serve as the requirement during design and the user guide after release.

**Work breakdown.** Objective, Outcome, Feature or Design, Backlog Item or Bug, Task. Features are tangible units of value a non-technical stakeholder understands and have a definition of ready and done. Backlog items are independently deployable and are created only when a feature is ready and near-term. ROMs support planning and are not commitments; estimates get more accurate as work gets nearer.

**Teams and roles.** Cross-functional delivery teams own an objective end to end. Each objective has a Product Owner (usually a product manager) who owns the canonical document and requirements, and an Engineering Owner who owns technical design and implementation. Test engineers, DevOps engineers, UX, business analysis and staff engineers work inside and across teams as consultants and enablers, not as gates. Team membership is stable but rotation happens as objectives change. Leadership is distributed.

**Quality.** Shared. Test-first mindset. Regression coverage is checked and added before a change is made, and software and test engineers pair on it. Tests sit at the right level of the pyramid; automation is the default; exploratory testing is deliberate and time-boxed. The same shared ownership applies to security, privacy, accessibility (WCAG AA), performance, reliability and operability. Every production change is peer reviewed and recorded. Engineering guardrails (small changes, conventional commits) are conversation triggers, not rules. Decisions with lasting impact are recorded.

**Release and deployment.** A deployment is any production change; a release is a change that affects users, usually enabled by configuration after deployment. Releases appear on a release dashboard that staff can subscribe to; deployments are logged with notice proportional to risk. Non-automated releases have a reviewed release plan with smoke tests and rollback.

**Operation and support.** A three-line service desk: first line in Customer Support, second line a rotating engineer supported by a shadow software engineer and shadow test engineer, third line in every delivery team with reserved capacity. Priorities P1 to P4 from impact and urgency. A P1 stops other work. A Known Error Log records conscious decisions not to fix yet. Root causes are categorised so trends feed back into planning. Lessons learned after incidents and milestones.

## 4. Design

### 4.1 Principles for the new Playbook

1. **One operating model, one vocabulary.** The Playbook uses the words on the roadmap and in Azure DevOps and nowhere invents its own. A glossary page defines them once.
2. **Principle here, procedure in the Knowledgebase.** If a page would need a repository name, an internal URL, a person's name or a step-by-step, the content moves. The Playbook links to the Knowledgebase section, never to a document.
3. **Short enough to read in an afternoon.** Target under 18,000 words and about 30 pages. One idea per page; a page rarely exceeds 600 words.
4. **Written cold.** Every page should make sense to someone who joined last week or has never worked here.
5. **Quality is a section, not a sub-folder.** Testing, security, reliability and operability sit together at the top level, framed as shared responsibility.
6. **AM tone of voice.** Short sentences, active voice, British English, no em dashes, no buzzwords.

### 4.7 Service desk in the Playbook

The support model is part of how the department works and says something about our culture, so its shape goes in the Playbook on the Operation and support page. The mechanics stay in the Service Desk folder on SharePoint and in the Knowledgebase.

In the Playbook (about 500 words):

- **One front door.** Every issue comes through Customer Support, who triage, prioritise and own communication with whoever raised it. Nobody brings an issue straight to an engineer.
- **Three lines.** First line in Customer Support. Second line is a rotating engineer, supported each week by a shadow lead software engineer and a shadow test engineer. Third line is every delivery team, with reserved capacity, pulling the highest-priority item it can resolve.
- **Priority from impact and urgency.** P1 to P4, with Customer Support deciding the order within a level. A P1 becomes the department's only priority until it is contained, and ends with a root cause analysis and lessons learned.
- **Known errors are decisions, not a parking lot.** An issue joins the Known Error Log only once it is understood and someone has consciously chosen not to fix it yet.
- **Support feeds planning.** Root causes are categorised so trends, regressions above all, shape objectives and the testing policy.
- **Measuring outcomes** after release and lessons learned after milestones.

Not in the Playbook: runbook steps, Halo and Azure DevOps field guidance, status definitions, the rota, notice periods, communication templates, named people.
